A single photo from a modern phone can be 3–8 MB. Attach a few to an email and it bounces. Drop them on a webpage and it takes forever to load. You need smaller files — but you don't need to hand your photos to a cloud service to get them.

## Why compress images at all

- **Email limits.** Most providers cap attachments around 20–25 MB total.
- **Page speed.** Google ranks faster sites higher, and images are usually the heaviest thing on a page.
- **Storage.** Compressed photos free up space without deleting memories.

## Compress locally, keep the quality

Our [image compressor](/tools/image-compressor) runs entirely in your browser:

1. Drop in your JPG or PNG (it also handles WebP and AVIF).
2. Pick a target size or quality level.
3. Preview the result and download.

Because it runs on your device, **the original photo is never uploaded**. You see a side-by-side preview so you can stop before quality drops.

## Tips for the best results

- **Start with JPG for photos.** PNG is lossless, so it compresses less — use JPG unless you need transparency.
- **Don't over-shrink.** 70–85% quality is usually invisible to the eye but cuts file size dramatically.
- **Resize first if needed.** A 4000×3000 photo destined for a 800px-wide blog column should be [resized](/tools/image-resize-crop), not just compressed.

If your source is an iPhone HEIC, [convert it to JPG first](/tools/heic-to-jpg), then compress. And if you're preparing product shots, our [background remover](/tools/ai-photo-cutout) pairs well before export.

## The private alternative to "cloud compressors"

Cloud tools often keep copies of what you upload "for a while." For innocuous screenshots that may be fine — for personal or work photos, doing it on your device removes the risk entirely.
